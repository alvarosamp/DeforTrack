\abstract{Reliable forest monitoring increasingly depends on deep learning models trained on high-quality annotated datasets, yet publicly available resources remain limited for high-resolution forest/non-forest semantic segmentation. This paper presents DeforTrack, a multi-source, large-scale, manually annotated RGB dataset developed specifically for deforestation monitoring applications. DeforTrack comprises 6,094 original images acquired from UAV surveys, satellite imagery, and public environmental repositories, and expands to 14,648 images after data augmentation. The dataset combines cross-platform imagery, fine-grained polygon-based pixel-level annotations, and diverse environmental conditions, while providing a quantitative characterization of image sources, annotation quality, and forest-cover distribution. Beyond dataset construction, DeforTrack establishes a task-oriented benchmark for evaluating both segmentation accuracy and deployment-oriented performance. We evaluate U-Net, Mask R-CNN, YOLOv8, and YOLOv11 using segmentation metrics (IoU and mAP) together with inference latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption. The results show that YOLOv8s achieves the highest overall segmentation accuracy, whereas YOLOv8n and YOLOv11n provide the best trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency, indicating their potential for near-real-time monitoring and future embedded deployment. By publicly releasing DeforTrack~\url{https://universe.roboflow.com/alvaro-z5qmu/floresta-wgiln}, this work provides a reproducible benchmark that supports the development, evaluation, and deployment of robust deep learning solutions for forest segmentation and operational deforestation monitoring.}

\section{Introduction}

Forest preservation is threatened by anthropogenic activities and natural disturbances, such as illegal logging, uncontrolled fires, and land exploitation~\cite{g1,g2,g3}. For instance, the Brazilian Amazon has experienced rising deforestation rates, which disrupt ecosystems and hinder conservation efforts. Thus, accurate and timely deforestation monitoring is essential to inform authorities, support environmental enforcement, and enable sustainable reforestation initiatives. Traditional deforestation monitoring methods, such as field-based surveys and visual interpretation of imagery, are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and often inadequate for large-scale or near real-time applications~\cite{g1, g2}. In contrast, remote sensing technologies, including satellite imagery and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), also known as drones, have emerged as scalable and cost-effective alternatives for observing forest change dynamics~\cite{g1, g4, g5, g6, g7, g8}. Temporal sequences of high-resolution images enable the tracking of deforestation by capturing pixel-level variations over time.


To process this growing volume of data, a range of computational methods has been applied. Early approaches relied on algebra-based techniques and classical machine learning classifiers, whereas recent advances have shifted toward deep learning, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), for semantic segmentation tasks~\cite{g1, g4, g5, g6, g7, g8}. Deep learning-based models achieve high accuracy in distinguishing between forested and deforested areas by classifying each pixel based on its spatial and spectral content, enhancing spatial precision and temporal consistency in environmental monitoring. Several CNN-based architectures have been deployed in remote sensing applications. U-Net, for instance, is widely used due to its encoder-decoder structure, which maintains local detail while capturing global context~\cite{g4,g5}. Mask R-CNN extends object detection methods by enabling instance-level segmentation and pixel-precise classification~\cite{g10,g11,g12}. Additionally, YOLO-based architectures are favored for real-time monitoring due to their balance of speed and accuracy~\cite{g10,g16,rs17183170}. Techniques such as data augmentation and the simulation of lighting variations, shadows, and atmospheric conditions further enhance model robustness and generalization.

Despite these advances, there remains a critical gap in the availability of task-specific datasets designed for high-resolution forest segmentation. Several existing platforms and datasets provide valuable resources for environmental monitoring and remote sensing analysis. However, they were developed with different objectives and do not meet the specific requirements for training semantic segmentation models. Global Forest Watch, for instance, offers large-scale forest monitoring products derived from satellite time series, including tree-cover loss information suitable for regional and global assessments~\cite{watch2002global}. Nevertheless, these datasets do not provide high-resolution RGB image patches with manual polygon-based masks, which are essential for training and evaluating deep learning segmentation models. Similarly, MapBiomas delivers annual land-use and land-cover maps based on Landsat imagery at 30 m spatial resolution, making it a valuable resource for long-term territorial and historical analyses~\cite{rosa2019mapbiomas}. However, it does not offer manually annotated RGB imagery at the fine-grained pixel level required for accurate forest/non-forest segmentation.

Other remote sensing datasets also diverge from the specific task of training semantic segmentation models for forest/non-forest tracking. BigEarthNet is a large-scale Sentinel benchmark designed for remote sensing image classification and retrieval, with land-cover labels automatically derived from reference maps rather than manually annotated for semantic segmentation~\cite{sumbul2019bigearthnet}. SpaceNet provides high-resolution satellite imagery with geospatial annotations, but its focus is predominantly on urban mapping applications, including building footprint extraction, road extraction, and change detection, rather than forest monitoring \cite{van2018spacenet}. Even datasets commonly used for deforestation detection, such as those based on MODIS or Landsat time series, generally operate at coarser spatial resolutions and rely on spectral indices or change-detection algorithms rather than pixel-level manual annotations for binary forest/non-forest classification.

Recently, we proposed an embedded remote sensing framework for deforestation monitoring using UAV-based imagery~\cite{g10}. The system employed deep learning models to locally segment aerial images into forested and deforested regions. A lightweight algorithm was introduced to calculate the percentage of forested area based on pixel counts and transmit this information, along with geolocation metadata, to a central node for temporal analysis. However, due to the limited availability of annotated training data, comprising just 224 images sourced via web scraping and external datasets, the segmentation models exhibited suboptimal performance in terms of mean average precision (mAP), which limited their practical deployment.

Based on that, this paper addresses the low quality of existing datasets for deforestation by introducing DeforTrack, a novel dataset comprising $6,094$ high-resolution, manually segmented images collected from drones, satellite platforms, and public environmental sources. The dataset encompasses a diverse range of forested and deforested landscapes under various environmental conditions, enabling robust training of deep learning models. The main contributions of this work are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Development of DeforTrack, a multi-source, high-resolution RGB dataset specifically designed for forest/non-forest semantic segmentation, combining drone, satellite, and public environmental imagery with manual polygon-based pixel-level annotations.
    
    \item Quantitative characterization of the dataset in terms of acquisition sources, geographic and environmental diversity, annotation protocol, and forest-cover distribution extracted from the segmentation masks.
    
    \item Performance evaluation of multiple semantic segmentation models trained on DeforTrack, covering segmentation accuracy, boundary quality, computational efficiency, and deployment-oriented metrics such as inference time, memory usage, and energy consumption.
\end{itemize}

DeforTrack is designed as a task-oriented benchmark for deep learning-based forest segmentation and deforestation monitoring. Its distinctiveness lies in the unique combination of several key features: high-resolution RGB image patches with manual polygon-based pixel-level annotations; a binary forest/non-forest segmentation protocol optimized for deforestation detection; images collected from multiple sources (drones, satellites, and public repositories) encompassing diverse environmental conditions and geographical contexts; and the combination of Brazilian UAV and Google Earth Engine (GEE) imagery with public forest and environmental imagery from multiple datasets, increasing visual diversity across vegetation structures, illumination conditions, terrain types, and non-forest
patterns. These features make DeforTrack the first dataset to combine these elements specifically for high-resolution forest segmentation, providing the research community with a dedicated resource for both accuracy evaluation and deployment-oriented assessments in real-world monitoring applications. \subsection{Data Collection}

The dataset comprises $640 \times 640$ RGB images collected from three main sources: drone-based aerial surveys, the Google Earth Engine platform~\cite{velastegui2023google}, and publicly available environmental image repositories. Some images were resized or adjusted to align with this resolution. From GEE, we extracted satellite images by selecting regions across Brazil with visible evidence of environmental degradation, such as deforestation scars, forest fragmentation, exposed soil, and transitions between forested and non-forested areas. The selected imagery spans different seasons, lighting conditions, vegetation patterns, and land-use configurations to enhance diversity and support model generalization. A total of $400$ satellite images were collected. We acquired drone-based imagery through planned UAV missions over forested and partially deforested areas in southeastern Brazil. UAVs were equipped with high-resolution RGB sensors and operated at altitudes ranging from $800$ to $1,200$ meters, simulating an orbital imaging perspective while minimizing atmospheric distortion. This process yielded $350$ images. 

Additionally, $5,344$ images were sourced from publicly available environmental monitoring repositories containing aerial and remote-sensing scenes of forested areas, vegetation cover, exposed soil, agricultural surroundings, and forest/non-forest transitions. These external images were included to increase the visual variability of the dataset, covering differences in vegetation structure, canopy density, terrain characteristics, illumination conditions, image acquisition perspective, and non-forest background patterns. These three sources contributed to an initial pool of $6,094$ original images capturing diverse forest structures, vegetation densities, terrain types, and anthropogenic disturbances. Overall, DeforTrack combines drone-based imagery, satellite imagery, and publicly available environmental images to provide a heterogeneous set of forest and non-forest scenes for training and evaluating high-resolution segmentation models under different visual conditions.

\subsection{Annotation Process}

All images were manually annotated using polygon-based pixel-level masks on the Roboflow platform~\cite{roboflow2026defortrack}, following a binary forest/non-forest labeling protocol. To assess the reliability of the manual annotation process, we conducted an inter-annotator agreement study on a validation subset comprising $5~\%$ of the dataset ($n=248$ images, after matching by original filename across annotators). This subset was independently annotated by three annotators, and the resulting polygon masks were rasterized to a common $640 \times 640$ canvas to enable pixel-wise comparison. For each pair of annotators, we computed the Intersection over Union (IoU), Dice/F1 coefficient~\cite{dice1945measures}, precision, and pixel-level Cohen's Kappa coefficient~\cite{wan2015kappa}, using one annotator as reference and the other as the compared mask. IoU, Dice/F1, precision, and Kappa are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over the $n=248$ image-level values, and the $95~\%$ confidence interval for the mean IoU was additionally estimated. Table~\ref{tab:interannotator} summarizes the results.
 
The mean pairwise IoU across the three annotator pairs was $74.55 \pm 19.44~\%$, with a mean Dice/F1 of $83.58 \pm 16.64~\%$ and a mean per-image Kappa of $0.727 \pm 0.216$. According to the Landis and Koch scale~\cite{landis1977}, Kappa values between $0.61$ and $0.80$ indicate good agreement, and the mean values for all three annotator pairs fall within this range, although a subset of images with extreme forest-cover ratios ($\approx 0~\%$ or $\approx 100~\%$) yielded lower or occasionally negative per-image Kappa values, since the coefficient is highly sensitive to class imbalance at the single-image level. We additionally computed a two-of-three majority-vote consensus mask for each image and measured each annotator's IoU against this consensus, obtaining $82.75~\%$, $86.67~\%$, and $89.72~\%$ for Annotator 1, Annotator 2, and Annotator 3, respectively. These results demonstrate a good level of agreement among annotators, consistent with the inherent ambiguity of forest/non-forest boundaries in fragmented or transitional landscapes, as discussed in Subsection~\ref{sec:limitations}. This quantitative assessment provides an empirical estimate of labeling quality and annotation uncertainty for the DeforTrack dataset.
 
\subsection{Data Augmentation and Availability}
\label{sec:data_augmentation}

We applied data augmentation techniques to the annotated training images, resulting in a final exported dataset with $14,648$ images, including original and augmented samples. The Roboflow augmentation configuration was set to generate three outputs per training example and included geometric transformations and photometric variations. Specifically, the applied
transformations were horizontal and vertical flipping, random cropping with $0~\%$ minimum zoom and $20~\%$ maximum zoom, affine shearing of up to $\pm 13^{\circ}$ in both horizontal and vertical directions, brightness adjustment between $-22~\%$ and $+22~\%$, Gaussian blur of up to $3.7$ pixels, and noise injection affecting up to $1.56~\%$ of pixels. These augmentations emulate real-world image variability caused by seasonal changes, illumination differences, acquisition conditions, and sensor artifacts, enhancing the robustness of models trained on the dataset. The Roboflow platform performed the data split before the data augmentation stage, reducing the risk that augmented versions of the same original file were replicated across different subsets. DeforTrack is partitioned into $12,861$ training images ($88~\%$), $895$ validation images ($6~\%$), and $892$ final test images ($6~\%$). The final test subset was held out from training and was used to report the segmentation results in Table~\ref{tab:segmentation_metrics}. These ratios correspond to the default Roboflow configuration and were maintained because they provide a large training subset while preserving independent validation and testing partitions. 

Although the split was performed before data augmentation, reducing the risk of direct augmentation leakage, this image-level partitioning does not fully guarantee spatial or temporal independence among the subsets. To further inspect potential overlap, we performed a filename-based analysis across the training, validation, and test partitions. This inspection identified $586$ normalized image names appearing across more than one subset. Since the normalization procedure removes augmentation-specific suffixes and export identifiers, these cases should be interpreted as potential, rather than definitive, split overlap. Nevertheless, this result indicates that complete independence among subsets cannot be guaranteed using filename inspection alone.
Therefore, although the current protocol reduces direct augmentation leakage, it does not fully ensure spatial or temporal independence. In remote sensing datasets, images acquired from nearby regions, similar acquisition campaigns, or close acquisition dates may share visual and spatial patterns. If such correlated samples are distributed across training, validation, and testing subsets, model performance may be overestimated.

\section{Deep-learning Semantic Segmentation Models Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art semantic segmentation models widely used in remote sensing and computer vision to assess the DeforTrack dataset. This section describes the training setup, segmentation pipeline, forest area estimation methodology, and performance metrics used for model benchmarking.

\subsection{Deep-learning models and training setup}

The evaluation encompassed multiple deep learning architectures selected based on their relevance to environmental monitoring tasks and suitability for embedded deployment. The tested models include U-Net, Mask R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network) (via Detectron2), and two versions of the YOLO (You Only Look Once) family (YOLOv8 and the recently proposed YOLOv11), with the notebooks publicly available\footnote{\url{https://github.com/alvarosamp/DeforTrack}}. 
For YOLO-based models, we considered five variants per version: nano, small, medium, large, and extra-large, capturing a range of trade-offs between computational load and accuracy. Similarly, multiple Mask R-CNN variants were evaluated, differing in backbone depth (ResNet-50 vs. ResNet-101) and head configuration (C4, DC5, and FPN). The U-Net model was included as a semantic-segmentation baseline due to its prevalence in geospatial segmentation tasks and its interpretable architecture. The supplied checkpoints were trained using Python 3 and an NVIDIA T4 GPU in Google Colab. The standardized re-evaluations reported in this revision were executed locally with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU with 8 GB of VRAM; therefore, computational measurements are hardware-specific and should not be interpreted as universal deployment values.

\subsection{Pixel-wise Mask Fusion for Forest Area Estimation}
\label{subsec:algorithm}

We applied a post-processing algorithm that aggregates multiple segmentation
masks to estimate the forested area. Each model output yields one or more binary
segmentation masks $M_k \in \{0,1\}^{m \times n}$, where a value of 1 indicates
the presence of forest at a given pixel coordinate. Figure~\ref{f2} exemplifies
the segmented mask output for YOLOv8x, YOLOv11s, Mask R-CNN, and U-Net. A final
segmentation mask $M_f$ is computed via a pixel-wise Boolean OR operation across
the $k$ masks.

\begin{equation}
    M_f = M^1 \lor M^2 \lor \cdots \lor M^k,
\end{equation}

where $\lor$ denotes the Boolean OR operator. Each element $M_{ij}^f$ of the
final mask is given by

\begin{equation}
M_{ij}^f = M_{ij}^1 \lor M_{ij}^2 \lor \cdots \lor M_{ij}^k.
\end{equation}

The union operation was adopted because each predicted instance mask represents
a candidate forest region. Therefore, a pixel is classified as forest if it is
included in at least one predicted forest mask. This strategy avoids discarding
valid forest pixels when multiple masks partially overlap or when a forest
region is split into more than one predicted instance. In contrast, an
intersection-based rule would be overly restrictive, since only pixels shared by
all masks would be retained. A weighted average was not adopted because it would
require calibrated mask confidence values and an additional thresholding step.

The resulting final mask $M_f$ is then used to compute the percentage of forest
cover $A_f$ in the image, given by:

\begin{equation}
    A_f = \frac{1}{m\times n}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} M_{ij}^f.
\end{equation}

This method provides a simple and efficient estimate of forest-cover percentage
from the segmentation outputs. In this work, the method was evaluated as a
post-processing step under the same computational setup used for model
benchmarking; deployment on resource-constrained embedded platforms remains
future work.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Model performance was quantitatively assessed using both accuracy-oriented and resource-efficiency metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intersection over Union (IoU):} Measures the overlap between the predicted mask $M_p$ and the ground truth mask $M_t$. It is defined as:
    \[
    \text{IoU} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} (M_{p_{ij}} \land M_{t_{ij}})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} (M_{p_{ij}} \lor M_{t_{ij}})},
    \]
    where $\land$ and $\lor$ represent the Boolean AND and OR operations, respectively. The mask used to calculate the IoU is the $M^f$ defined in (1).
    \item \textbf{Mean Average Precision (mAP):} Evaluates the overall detection and localization performance by averaging the precision across multiple IoU thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Inference Time and FPS:} Represent the average time (in seconds) required to segment a single image and the effective throughput in frames per second, respectively.
    \item \textbf{Computational Resource Metrics:} Include peak RAM usage, CPU utilization, and estimated energy consumption during inference. These metrics support the assessment of model deployability on real-time and embedded systems.
    %They allow for assessing the deployability of models on real-time and embedded systems.
\end{itemize}

The evaluations were performed on the same final test subset of 892 images to ensure consistency and fairness across model comparisons. 
For mAP, we report mAP@50 and mAP@50:95, where the latter corresponds to IoU thresholds ranging from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05. 
%Regarding the mAP, we set an IoU threshold of 0.5 and a second metric with it varying between 0.5 and 0.95.
RAM usage, CPU utilization, inference time, FPS, and energy consumption were measured using the same profiling procedure for all evaluated models. CPU and
RAM were monitored with \texttt{psutil}, while GPU power consumption was sampled
with NVML and converted to energy by multiplying the average power by the
inference time.

To provide uncertainty estimates and improve repeatability analysis, we computed confidence intervals for the main image-level metrics. For each metric, the mean value was computed over the validation images, and the $95~\%$ confidence interval was estimated as:
\begin{equation}
CI_{95\%} = \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}},
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $s$ is the standard deviation across test images, and $n$ is the number of evaluated images. This analysis provides an estimate of the uncertainty associated with the reported average performance and complements the single-point values reported in the comparison tables.

\section{Results and Discussion}

This section presents a comprehensive performance analysis of the evaluated models, which were trained on the DeforTrack dataset. Tables~\ref{tab:segmentation_metrics} and~\ref{tab:computational_metrics} summarize the metrics across all model variants. Among the YOLOv8 series, the YOLOv8s variant achieved the highest segmentation accuracy, with an IoU of $88.98~\%$, an mAP of $91.34~\%$, Dice/F1 of $93.62~\%$, and Pixel Accuracy of  $95.95~\%$. This result indicates that YOLOv8s provided a strong balance between segmentation quality and computational efficiency. In contrast, YOLOv8x presented a lower IoU of $70.93~\%$, despite its larger model size of $274.25$~MB. This behavior suggests that increasing model complexity did not necessarily improve the segmentation performance for the evaluated dataset. The YOLOv8n variant achieved the highest throughput among the YOLOv8 models, with $47.71$ FPS and an inference time of $21.40$~ms, making it suitable for latency-sensitive or resource-constrained applications.

The YOLOv11 variants followed a similar accuracy--efficiency pattern. YOLOv11m obtained the highest IoU within the YOLOv11 family, reaching $88.36~\%$, whereas YOLOv11x achieved the best boundary-oriented performance, with Boundary IoU of $49.14~\%$ and Boundary F1 of $71.39~\%$. YOLOv11n was the smallest model, with $5.75$ MB, and achieved $43.58$ FPS with an inference time of
$23.30$ ms. Overall, YOLOv11n provides a compact profile for near-real-time monitoring and potential future embedded deployment, while YOLOv8s showed the strongest segmentation accuracy.

They further illustrate model behavior under additional visual conditions, including high forest-cover regions, continuous canopy, forest boundaries, heterogeneous vegetation, and fragmented canopy patterns. Since complete image-level biome metadata is not available for all samples in the final exported dataset, these examples should be interpreted as visually distinct forest and land-cover scenarios rather than confirmed biome-specific samples.

\subsection{Limitations and Generalization Considerations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Although the quantitative and qualitative results indicate that YOLO-based models achieved the best trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, some limitations must be considered. Segmentation errors are more likely to occur under shadows, strong illumination variation, low contrast, sparse vegetation, dry vegetation, and mixed land-cover patterns. These conditions can alter the apparent color and texture of vegetation, reducing the boundary definition between forest and non-forest regions. The most difficult cases occur near transition regions between forest and non-forest areas, where boundaries are visually ambiguous. Fragmented landscapes, exposed soil, agricultural areas, roads, shadows, and low vegetation density may present visual patterns similar to forest canopy or partially preserved vegetation. These conditions can lead to false positives, when non-forest regions are predicted as forest, or false negatives, when sparse, low, or degraded forest areas are not detected. This limitation becomes relevant for biome-specific generalization, since some forest types may present lower canopy height, lower vegetation density, or less homogeneous green texture than the examples most represented in the training set.

Another limitation concerns the geographic and biome-level characterization of the final exported dataset. Although DeforTrack was designed to support Brazilian deforestation monitoring and includes a predominance of Brazilian forest scenarios, the dataset was assembled from multiple private and public sources, including environmental image repositories from different regions. Since the final version does not preserve complete image-level metadata about geographic origin, acquisition source, or biome, it is not possible to reliably assign every sample to a specific Brazilian biome. Therefore, the qualitative examples presented in this work should be interpreted as visually distinct forest and land-cover scenarios rather than as confirmed biome-specific samples.

Regarding generalization, the current split does not fully demonstrate geographic or temporal generalization. Although the split was performed before data augmentation, reducing direct leakage from identical augmented samples, a strict geographic, temporal, or source-aware split was not applied. Therefore, spatially close, visually similar, or temporally related samples may still share patterns across training, validation, and test subsets. This limitation may lead to optimistic performance estimates in some scenarios. 

Future work will include more systematic failure-case analysis and source-aware, region-wise, biome-aware, and temporal validation protocols. In such protocols, images should be grouped by acquisition source, geographic region, biome, flight campaign, or acquisition date before partitioning. This would provide a stricter evaluation of model robustness and its ability to generalize to unseen regions,
biomes, and acquisition conditions.


\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper introduced DeforTrack, a large-scale, high-resolution, manually annotated dataset designed to advance deep learning-based forest segmentation and deforestation monitoring. The dataset comprises 14,648 images after augmentation, derived from 6,094 original images acquired from UAV surveys, satellite imagery, and public environmental repositories. Beyond presenting the dataset itself, this work provides a comprehensive characterization of its acquisition sources, annotation protocol, and forest-cover distribution, demonstrating substantial variability in vegetation density and environmental conditions suitable for robust model development and evaluation.

Extensive benchmarking of U-Net, Mask R-CNN, YOLOv8, and YOLOv11 showed that modern YOLO-based architectures achieve the best balance between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, making them particularly suitable for real-time and embedded remote sensing applications. In addition, the proposed evaluation framework extends beyond conventional segmentation metrics by incorporating deployment-oriented measurements, including inference latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption, providing a more comprehensive assessment of practical model suitability.

Although future work should adopt source-aware, geographically stratified, biome-aware, and temporally independent validation protocols, the current dataset already provides a valuable benchmark for developing and comparing semantic segmentation methods for forest monitoring. By making DeforTrack publicly available, this work establishes a reproducible resource that supports future advances in data-driven environmental monitoring and facilitates the development of robust deep learning solutions for operational deforestation assessment.
